# Public Speaking Curriculum

This curriculum contains 6 public speaking models delivered one per week. Each model builds on the previous one.

---

## Week 1: PREP Method

**Core Principles:**
- **P**oint: State your main point clearly and directly at the start.
- **R**eason: Explain why your point matters — the "because".
- **E**xample: Support your point with a concrete story, data, or anecdote.
- **P**oint: Restate your main point to reinforce it and close.

**Evaluation Criteria:**
1. Does the opening clearly state a single main point?
2. Is there a clear reason/rationale immediately following the point?
3. Is there at least one concrete example (story, statistic, or analogy)?
4. Does the script conclude by restating the main point?

**Worked Example:**
> *Point:* Remote work improves employee productivity.
> *Reason:* Without a commute, employees save an average of 90 minutes a day they can redirect to focused work.
> *Example:* In a Stanford study of 16,000 employees, those working from home showed a 13% increase in output compared to their office counterparts.
> *Point:* That is why embracing remote work is one of the smartest investments a company can make in its people.

## Week 2: Monroe's Motivated Sequence

**Core Principles:**
- **Attention**: Hook the audience immediately — a question, a startling fact, or a short story.
- **Need**: Establish that a problem exists and that it affects the audience.
- **Satisfaction**: Present your solution to the problem.
- **Visualization**: Paint a vivid picture of the world with and without the solution.
- **Action**: Give the audience a clear, specific call to action.

**Evaluation Criteria:**
1. Does the opening capture attention within the first two sentences?
2. Is the problem stated in a way that makes it feel personal to the audience?
3. Is the solution clearly described?
4. Is there a contrast between the "before" and "after" states?
5. Is there a clear, actionable request at the close?

**Worked Example:**
> *Attention:* Every 40 seconds, someone in the world takes their own life.
> *Need:* Mental health support at work is still stigmatised — 60% of employees never disclose struggles to their manager for fear of career consequences.
> *Satisfaction:* We can change this by training every manager in active listening and mental health first aid.
> *Visualization:* Imagine a workplace where asking for help is as normal as asking for a budget code — and where productivity thrives because people feel safe.
> *Action:* Sign up for the 3-hour mental health first aid training next Thursday. Your team needs it.

## Week 3: Minto Pyramid (Pyramid Principle)

**Core Principles:**
- **Conclusion first**: Lead with your answer or recommendation, not the background.
- **Supporting arguments**: Present 2–4 key arguments that support the conclusion.
- **Evidence**: Back each argument with specific data, examples, or facts.

**Evaluation Criteria:**
1. Is the main conclusion or recommendation stated in the opening sentence or paragraph?
2. Are there distinct, clearly labelled supporting arguments?
3. Does each argument have at least one piece of evidence?
4. Is background/context kept minimal and placed after the conclusion?

**Worked Example:**
> *Conclusion:* We should migrate our CRM to a cloud platform this quarter.
> *Argument 1:* Cost savings are significant — cloud CRM reduces infrastructure costs by 40%.
> *Evidence:* Our current on-premise licence and maintenance total €180K/year; cloud equivalent is €108K.
> *Argument 2:* Reliability improves — cloud platforms offer 99.9% uptime SLAs versus our current 97%.
> *Evidence:* Last year we experienced 26 hours of unplanned downtime, costing an estimated €52K in lost sales.
> *Argument 3:* The team can deploy faster — new features go live in days, not months.
> *Evidence:* Our competitor launched a mobile quoting tool in 6 weeks using the same platform we are evaluating.

## Week 4: STAR Method (Storytelling)

**Core Principles:**
- **S**ituation: Set the scene — where, when, who, and what was the context.
- **T**ask: Describe the challenge or goal you faced.
- **A**ction: Explain the specific steps you took (use "I" not "we").
- **R**esult: Share the outcome with specific, measurable impact.

**Evaluation Criteria:**
1. Is the situation vivid and specific enough to picture?
2. Is the task (the problem or goal) clearly stated?
3. Are the actions described in the first person and specific?
4. Is the result quantified or clearly tangible?

**Worked Example:**
> *Situation:* In March 2023, our team had just lost our lead developer two weeks before a client launch.
> *Task:* I was responsible for ensuring the product shipped on schedule with no loss of quality.
> *Action:* I reorganised the sprint into three parallel workstreams, temporarily onboarded two contractors, and held daily 15-minute stand-ups to catch blockers early. I also negotiated a 72-hour scope reduction with the client for one non-critical feature.
> *Result:* We launched on the original date with zero critical bugs. The client rated the delivery 9/10 and extended the contract for a further year.

## Week 5: SBI Feedback Model (Situation–Behaviour–Impact)

**Core Principles:**
- **S**ituation: Describe the specific situation or context when the behaviour occurred.
- **B**ehaviour: Describe the observable behaviour only — no judgement or interpretation.
- **I**mpact: Explain the impact the behaviour had on you, the team, or the outcome.

**Evaluation Criteria:**
1. Is a specific situation named (time, place, or event)?
2. Is the behaviour described factually, without adjectives like "lazy" or "unprofessional"?
3. Is the impact stated from the speaker's perspective using "I" statements?
4. Is the script free of generalisations ("you always", "you never")?

**Worked Example:**
> *Situation:* In yesterday's client presentation, when we were presenting the Q3 roadmap…
> *Behaviour:* …you interrupted the client twice while they were asking questions and redirected the conversation before they had finished speaking.
> *Impact:* I noticed the client looked frustrated and stopped asking questions after that. I am concerned it may have weakened their confidence in our partnership.

## Week 6: Rule of Three

**Core Principles:**
- Organise every speech around exactly **three main ideas**.
- Each idea should be roughly equal in weight and parallel in structure.
- Open by previewing the three ideas; close by restating them.

**Evaluation Criteria:**
1. Are there exactly three main ideas (no more, no fewer)?
2. Are the three ideas stated in parallel grammatical form?
3. Does the opening preview all three ideas?
4. Does the closing restate all three ideas?
5. Is each idea supported with at least one example or piece of evidence?

**Worked Example:**
> *Opening:* A great leader does three things: they listen before they speak, they decide with data, and they celebrate their team's wins.
> *Point 1:* Great leaders listen before they speak. They hold space for dissent in every meeting and paraphrase before responding.
> *Point 2:* Great leaders decide with data. They build dashboards for every key decision and question assumptions with evidence.
> *Point 3:* Great leaders celebrate their team's wins. They name individuals publicly, write personal thank-you notes, and share credit generously.
> *Closing:* Remember: listen first, decide with data, and celebrate your people — that is the formula for exceptional leadership.
